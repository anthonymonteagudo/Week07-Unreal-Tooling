UI/UX research · from artist goals to tested tools

12 October 2026 · Research supplement · Suggested edits, not an edited PowerPoint

Recommendation: keep the assessment → overview → peer-testing sequence. Add a small design process around the existing usability principles: define the artist’s task, compare rough layouts, observe a realistic attempt, then revise. Use psychology to explain decisions throughout.

Start with these four resources

  1. Jodie Azhar’s GDC slides, PDF pages 32–35: closest immediately usable slide material for artist-tool layout.
  2. Apple: Principles of great design: a short 17:16 overview; prioritize agency, familiarity, and simplicity.
  3. NN/g: Paper Prototyping, then Usability Testing 101: preparation for the classroom method.
  4. Chad Moore: UX Applied to Technical Art: direct professional context. Follow with Laura Teeples for the wider pipeline.

GDC examples concern game-development tools but are not all Unreal-specific. Apply them alongside the existing Fortnite, Lyra, and Unreal batch-renaming cases. Apple and Figma supply transferable design methods, not Unreal implementation instructions.

Teaching additions and field-level proposals

14B · User stories

Describe the artist’s goal before designing the panel

Placement: Proposed insertion after 14; before scope/layout examples.

  • Who is using it?
  • What are they trying to accomplish—and why?
  • What would count as success?

Unreal/tech-art example: As an environment artist, I want to bring a revised prop from Maya into Unreal so I can inspect it in the level without rebuilding its setup. This is an illustrative story, not a verified user requirement.

Read-aloud notes

A user story helps us discuss the job this tool should support. It is not a request for a particular button. Ask what the artist is doing now and where the difficulty occurs. Then turn the goal into things we can observe: the intended asset is updated, the result can be located, and unrelated work stays unchanged. What would you ask this artist before building anything?

Application: Write role, goal, benefit; add one observable success condition and one thing that must not change. Keep the full story-writing exercise for tool planning if peer preparation is already full.

Visual candidate: A one-line story beside a short Maya → export → Unreal → inspect journey. Proposed course example, not a published studio workflow.

14C · Sketching and Figma

Compare layouts before committing to one

Placement: Proposed insertion before 15; combine with grouping rather than adding a long software tutorial.

  • Sketch two ways to support the same task
  • Show initial, result, and error states
  • Test what the artist thinks they can do

Unreal/tech-art example: For the same importer, compare a compact source/destination panel with a staged review flow. Neither is automatically better. Show where the artist would detect a wrong destination.

Read-aloud notes

You can test an idea before writing the tool. Sketch two arrangements and ask a partner where they would start and what they expect next. Include a result and an error state; a single polished rectangle does not show the interaction. Paper is enough. Figma can connect these states into a clickable prototype. It still cannot prove that an Unreal import works. Which uncertainty could you test with a sketch?

Application: Instructor shows two rough alternatives and asks for a prediction. A full student sketch-and-test exercise needs a separate 3–5 minute allocation; it is not included in the proposed 20-minute route.

Visual candidate: R1 Figure 1, PDF page 1, illustrates serial versus parallel process. F1 contains connected-frame illustrations. Link to originals; no open artwork license verified.

14A · Psychology

Your mental model is not your user’s mental model

Placement: Fold into existing scope slide 14A.

  • Ask what the user expects
  • Make scope and state visible
  • Prefer recognition over remembered syntax

Unreal/tech-art example: An artist may expect an operation on one selected Actor to affect only that placement. If it changes a shared asset, the interface needs to communicate the wider scope.

Read-aloud notes

You know how your code works. Your classmate brings a different picture of what the tool does, shaped by tools they already use. Before the action, ask what they expect to change. Afterward, compare that expectation with the result. Show the target and relevant state instead of requiring them to remember hidden context. Where might your tool’s behavior surprise another artist?

Application: Ask the tester to predict the affected objects before running the operation. Treat their explanation as evidence about expectations, not evidence that the implementation is correct.

Visual candidate: P1 includes a mental-model cycle; use as a linked instructor reference. Pair with the existing renamer result, rather than adding an unrelated consumer UI.

15A / 15B · Apple and attention

Help the artist act confidently

Placement: Weave into feedback and recovery slides; no separate Apple-history lecture.

  • Use familiar behavior for familiar actions
  • Put routine feedback near the work
  • Make consequences and recovery clear

Unreal/tech-art example: An import summary can show changed, skipped, and failed items with paths. A consequential overwrite may need an explicit review step. Confirmations should not interrupt every harmless action.

Read-aloud notes

Apple’s guidance gives us a useful question: does this information deserve to interrupt the person? Routine progress can stay beside the operation. A consequential action may need a clearer review. Familiar controls also carry expectations: if something looks like an ordinary selection list, people expect it to behave like one. In Unreal, start with conventions artists already know. What feedback needs their attention now?

Application: Contrast a routine completion message with a partial-failure report. Ask which needs a next action and why. Retain keyboard focus, readable labels, and noncolor cues from 15B.

Visual candidate: A1 chapter links provide candidate video excerpts. G3 PDF pages 32–35 provide real tool-layout material. Prepare and inspect any chosen frame before placing it in the deck.

16 · Testing and feedback

Test throughout development; revise from evidence

Placement: Expand the existing peer-test transition rather than adding a detached testing lecture.

  • Task → observe → interpret → change → retest
  • Separate observations from explanations
  • Record the version and help given

Unreal/tech-art example: Observation: the tester opened the wrong destination. Interpretation: the destination label may be unclear. Proposed change: show the full target path. Retest: can a new tester select the intended target without help?

Read-aloud notes

Feedback is most useful when it changes what we do next. Watch the task before asking for preferences. Record what happened, then separate your explanation from the observation. A sketch can test interpretation; a running tool can test the actual operation. After you change the tool, test again. In today’s three rounds, keep the version stable so you can compare observations; plan the revision and retest afterward. What evidence would persuade you that your fix worked?

Application: Use the same task across the three peer rounds. Record any emergency fix and changed version; do not treat results across versions as directly comparable. Three classmates provide useful leads, not proof that the tool is universally usable.

Visual candidate: U5 has iterative-design diagrams. Link to the source; NN/g artwork permission is not implied by free reading access.

Psychology: keep it concrete

Teach live: mental models explain predictions; recognition reduces memory demands; grouping and hierarchy direct attention. Refer back to the existing proximity source for layout. Connect each idea to a visible tool decision and a question for the tester.

Avoid unsupported shortcuts: do not teach “seven controls maximum,” “three clicks maximum,” or “five users find everything.” Do not infer universal productivity gains from one paper. Appearance, familiarity, and confidence are useful to discuss but do not establish task success. Fitts’s law, Hick–Hyman law, and aesthetic-usability research could support later depth; their primary evidence has not been reviewed in this pass.

Small additions to the lab prompts

  • Before the overview: preserve the first answer to “What would you change first, and why?”
  • After the overview: “Who is this for? What should they accomplish? Which initial judgment would you now revise or defend?”
  • During peer testing: “What did the tester expect? What did they do? What help did they need? Which conclusion is still only a hypothesis?”
  • After testing: “What will you change, and how will you check that the change helped?”

These are proposed refinements to existing prompts, not extra required worksheet sections. The current short printable sheets remain unchanged.

R1 · Research paper · Start here

Parallel Prototyping Leads to Better Design Results, More Divergence, and Increased Self-Efficacy

Dow et al. · ACM TOCHI · 2010

A study of 33 participants designing web advertisements compared parallel and serial prototyping with equal time and feedback. The parallel condition performed better on the reported design outcomes. Teaching inference: compare a few layout alternatives before polishing one.

Access and review status: Free author manuscript; methods, results, and discussion inspected. Participants were not professional designers; task was ad design, not editor-tool usability. No numerical gain transferred to Unreal.

R2 · Research paper · Further study

Beyond the five-user assumption: Benefits of increased sample sizes in usability testing

Laura Faulkner · 2003

The published abstract reports substantial variation in problems found by five-person samples drawn from 60 users. Small tests can help without guaranteeing that all important problems have been found.

Access and review status: Free abstract only; full publisher article paywalled. Not required reading or a free full-paper recommendation.
