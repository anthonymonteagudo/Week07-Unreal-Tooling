Tool assessment and three peer tests

Recommended draft for approximately 24 students: six groups of four. Begin with an eight-minute assessment of a fictional tool, then use the same evidence prompts in three reciprocal peer tests. Each student tests three classmates’ tools or prototypes and receives three reviews.

The demo block belongs primarily to the co-teacher and TA. The instructor’s two-minute showcase introduces the finished result of the Week 7 tool sequence. Presenter slots include their handoffs and questions. This plan proposes activities and materials; it does not revise the slides, assignment requirements, or grading policy.

Expanded UI/UX sequence proposal: assessment → 20-minute UI/UX overview/preparation → immediate peer testing. This supersedes the ten-minute teaching recommendation below, but its extra ten minutes still need allocation within the confirmed session. The table remains the earlier timing baseline; no contributor slot or peer round has been shortened. The revised proposal places the break before the assessment lab and preserves students’ initial judgments for comparison.

Activity 1: assess a batch renamer

Use a paper case, not a new software installation. The tool, asset names, and reported output are invented for this exercise. Students assess recorded behavior; this does not simulate a full usability test. The case deliberately includes a scope error and a repeated-prefix error, while leaving recovery unknown.

Give one group sheet to each group. Assign a reader, recorder, evidence checker, and reporter. The reader states the task; the recorder writes; the evidence checker compares every row; the reporter shares the finding. Students do not need to know Unreal’s naming rules: the exercise supplies its own rule.

Read aloud

“Your group is reviewing a tool that claims to rename selected assets. Decide what the result should be before I show you what happened. Then use the evidence to tell the developer what needs attention. You do not have to guess how the code works.”

Instructor answer key

Expected names are SM_crate_A, SM_crate_B, SM_crate_C, and unchanged barrel_D. The revealed result adds an extra prefix to the already-compliant selected asset and renames the unselected asset. The first two rows are correct.

Prioritize the unselected change when discussing scope: it touches work outside the task. Accept a reasoned alternative priority. A strong finding is: “With only the three crate assets selected, Rename also changed barrel_D to SM_barrel_D. The tool affected an unselected asset.” A useful retest repeats the same selection and verifies all four names, including the unchanged barrel.

The success message says four assets were updated. It reports a count but does not establish that the requested task succeeded. The evidence does not establish Undo support, filesystem behavior, speed, or the internal cause. Students should mark those as unknown. An additional test could run the operation twice and check for duplicate prefixes, but that second run has not been observed.

Five-minute fallback: 1 minute predict, 1 minute reveal, 2 minutes write one finding, 1 minute share. If there are fewer than five minutes, fold the case into the UI/UX segment; skip the optional additional-test question rather than rushing students through every field.

The UI/UX bridge · earlier ten-minute allocation

For the expanded teaching route, use the revised 20-minute slide draft. Preserve the first diagnosis before introducing principles, then revisit it and apply the same observation method in peer testing. The clock times in the following paragraph belong to the earlier baseline.

Use 5:10–5:13 to revisit the lab’s scope and output errors. Use 5:13–5:16 for a concise observation model: task, expected result, actual behavior, help needed, recovery. Use 5:16–5:20 for students to prepare their own short task and start state. A task states the goal and inputs, without prescribing clicks. Authors can prefill their tool name, author name, task, and protected scope on their three review sheets during this preparation window, leaving the observation and feedback sections to reviewers.

Example: “Arrange these three selected props into a row at 200-unit spacing. Leave the light and unselected props unchanged.” Students must adapt this to what their own tool actually supports. Test a small operation that can finish in about two minutes, not the whole project.

Activity 2: three reciprocal peer reviews

Make six groups of four and assign A, B, C, D. Where practical, distribute runnable tools across groups without publicly ranking students. One working laptop per pair is enough because each tool runs on its author’s prepared machine. Avoid account logins, installs, file transfers, or plugin setup during rounds. Group members can move chairs or switch to the other author’s laptop.

In each pair, the second letter tests the first author’s tool first. Then swap. Each student operates a peer’s tool, writes one sheet for that tool, and receives a sheet about their own. Across three rounds, each student writes three sheets and receives three, from distinct classmates.

Keep the same primary task across all three rounds to compare first-use behavior. A reviewer can choose a different follow-up test, but do not force a different theme each round. Record an issue as “not observed” when the available task did not expose it. A pass does not require inventing a fault: identify what worked and one next test.

Read aloud

“Each of you will test three classmates’ tools and receive three reviews. Give your partner a goal, then let them attempt it. Say what you expect as you work. Authors, note where you needed to help. Reviewers, describe what happened and what evidence supports your conclusion. We are looking for useful next steps.”

Participation when a tool is unfinished

Everyone remains a reviewer. Each author chooses the most complete thing they can share. The sheet records the review mode so design feedback is not mistaken for a successful software test.

A broken launch gets at most one minute of the test window before switching to an available fallback. Write “blocked” and what was visible. Do not spend the round debugging or install software. An author can receive valuable feedback on a prototype without implying that unfinished work meets any assessment requirement.

Attendance adjustments

The four-person group is what makes three distinct classmates possible. For 23 students, five groups of four plus a three-student group and an instructor can use the same pairing pattern. Those three students receive two student reviews and one staff review, clearly labeled. Do not count repeated partners as independent reviewers. Confirm attendance before publishing group assignments. If tools are scarce, keep the pairing structure and use prototype reviews; do not have four students watch one person while calling it four hands-on tests.

Feedback quality and final reflection

Replace “the UI is confusing” with “I selected three props, but the preview did not say how many would change; I asked the author before continuing.” Preserve a strength such as a readable before/after preview. Separate a witnessed problem from a suggested feature or an untested suspicion.

Use 5:55–5:57 to read the three received sheets. Use 5:57–5:59 to choose a priority using consequence and evidence, not simply vote count: one unintended edit may matter more than three cosmetic preferences. Use 5:59–6:00 to write the starting state, action, and expected evidence for a retest. No public report from all 24 students is needed in these five minutes.

Materials and instructor preparation

  • Six copies of the group sheet, plus a few spares; project the result reveal only after prediction.
  • 72 copies of the one-page peer sheet, plus spares: three per student. Each reviewer writes a sheet and gives it to the tool author immediately.
  • 24 copies of the reflection/concept sheet. The concept portion is only needed by students preparing a paper fallback.
  • Six group labels, A–D seats or cards, visible timer, and a projected pairing schedule.
  • Before rounds, confirm each student has a short task, declared success criterion, safe sample data, and a way to restore the starting state. Author support for domain knowledge is allowed and recorded.
  • Suggested staffing: instructor facilitates and calls time; co-teacher supports groups 1–3; TA supports groups 4–6. Confirm their availability; these are proposed roles.
  • Rehearse the group case in eight minutes and a reciprocal test in ten. This draft has not been classroom-trialed.

The instructor can circulate and note recurring issues without collecting every paper. The proposal creates no new submission, grade, or deadline. Decide later whether the reflection belongs in an existing check-in.

Relationship to existing materials

The earlier two-page worksheet remains a useful extended checklist. The new short sheet limits required writing to what fits the timed rounds.
